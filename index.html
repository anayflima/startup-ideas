Company of companies

Build many products inside one company and run them all on a shared AI machine.

A venture-funded company of companies. We build several products inside one company, each aiming for millions and then tens of millions in ARR. A shared AI company machine runs GTM, onboarding, support and ops for all of them. We keep the companies and don't sell the machine.
$570MRippling ARR, built as many products in parallel
$1.31BBending Spoons revenue on one shared stack
55+apps Zoho runs on one platform
$3B+raised by Thrive Holdings for AI-run portfolios

How it works

1

Pick a focused market that pays for software and is too small for the AI labs to care about.

2

A small pod does the 0 to 1: customer calls, product shape, first users.

3

The machine runs it: landing page, LinkedIn, outbound, onboarding, support, ops.

4

Double down or kill: PMF earns people and money, no traction gets shut down fast.

Why now

  • Big horizontal TAMs are where the labs go next (a lab already shipped a Granola-type notes app). Focused markets stay below their radar.
  • AI made building cheap. Running a company is now the bottleneck, and that is what the machine does.
  • We're funded, so we can run several bets in parallel and hire a platform team early.

Later: buy companies

With cash flow, buy stagnant software companies (they sell for about 3.5 to 5.5x ARR) and move them onto the machine, like Bending Spoons does with Evernote, WeTransfer and Vimeo.

Why it could work

  • Many shots on goal; one failed product doesn't kill the company.
  • The machine compounds: every launch makes the next one cheaper and faster.
  • Focused markets avoid head-on fights with the labs.
  • Investors already fund this shape (Rippling, Bending Spoons, Thrive Holdings).

What could go wrong

  • Distribution is the hard part. The machine speeds up execution, not finding demand.
  • Focus: several half-built products instead of one great one.
  • The portfolio must add up to a venture-size outcome ($100M+ ARR).
  • Burn grows fast if the kill rules are soft.

Open questions

  • Same buyer for every product (like Rippling) or unrelated markets (like Bending Spoons)?
  • One brand or many brands under one parent?
  • How many pods at once: 3, 5 or 8?
  • When do acquisitions start?

Next steps

  • Pick 3 to 5 focused B2B markets to test (filter in Playbook).
  • Decide the first machine pieces: GTM machine, auto onboarding, self-healing support.
  • Talk to 2 or 3 multi-product operators (lempire, saas.group, Bending Spoons alumni).

Rules of thumb

  • SaaS at $1-10M ARR usually runs at 20-40% profit.
  • Small SaaS sells for about 3.5-5.5x ARR (or 3-5x profit).
  • With AI running GTM and support, a product could need only 1 to 2 people.

Example

5 products at $2M ARR each = $10M ARR, about $2-4M profit a year and worth roughly $35-55M. At venture scale the goal is 10+ products, or a few that reach $20M+ each.

A draft of how a new product goes from idea to running on the machine.

A market passes when

  • B2B buyers already pay for software or services in it.
  • There's a reachable channel: a community, marketplace, SEO or a clear outbound list.
  • Too focused for the labs, big enough for $5-20M ARR.
  • Testable in 4 to 6 weeks.
  • Fits our backgrounds or shares a buyer with another product.

The company machine

  • GTM: landing page, LinkedIn page, daily posts, outbound, ads.
  • Auto onboarding and product tours.
  • Self-healing support and ops agents.
  • Shared auth, billing, analytics and one dashboard.
  • A launch template for every new product.

Stages and gates

1

Explore, 2-4 weeks: 20+ customer calls, a landing page, a waitlist or letters of intent.

2

Build, 4-6 weeks: MVP on shared auth, billing and onboarding; first paying users.

3

Prove, ~90 days: no paying customers or no MRR growth means kill it and keep the learnings.

4

Scale: steady MRR growth and good retention earn a bigger pod.

5

Run: the machine runs it; humans move to the next product.

Capital rules (our assumptions)

  • Fixed budget per pod per stage; money follows the gates.
  • At about $10M a year of spend: platform team $3-4M, 6 to 8 pods at $0.5-0.8M each.
  • Acquisitions use a hurdle rate, like Constellation (~20-25% IRR).

Planning assumptions, not researched numbers.

Gohar's question

"Was this what Pentagon was doing?" She saw the same pattern here (an AI that runs a business with named agents per department). To check: how Pentagon compares to The Company Company.

thecompany.company sells an AI layer that runs a business. It's the closest product to our machine.

What they do

  • "The AI that runs your business": a team plus always-on agents with shared memory and all your tools.
  • The company is drawn as departments (Engineering, Product, GTM, Operations) with named agents next to humans.
  • Agents read 212 replies to a welcome email, write specs, close the books, refund charges and ship code; humans approve.
  • Sold as SaaS: Individual and Teams plans (Pro, Max, Ultra).

What it means for us

  • A funded team is betting agents can run every department.
  • They sell the shovels; we mine. They earn from the platform, we earn from the companies it runs.
  • We could use tools like this inside our machine instead of building every piece.
  • Risk: if our machine gets very good, selling it is tempting, which puts us in their market.

AWS for agent automations

Cloud infrastructure that runs AI agents and automations for you.

Nobody should need a Mac mini running OpenClaw 24/7. Set up "every day do X, Y, Z" online; it runs on our servers and the results come to you.

Why it could work

  • Real pain and recurring revenue.
  • Fits our ML and infra background.
  • Growing market; can start narrow, e.g. one-click hosted OpenClaw.

What could go wrong

  • The big labs are already there.
  • Crowded and generic without a wedge.
  • Capital and ops heavy, with thin margins between LLM costs and cheap VPSs.
  • Trust and security of logins on our servers.

Where it fits

Hard to win as a standalone horizontal product. It works best as a narrow vertical (one persona, ready-made automations, Swiss/EU hosting) or as the infrastructure inside the company of companies machine.

Products that already run agents and automations in the cloud.

Hosted OpenClaw

OpenClaw Cloud, V2 Cloud, HoopyClaw, or simply a cheap VPS.

Developer infra

Modal, E2B, Trigger.dev: sandboxes and job runners for developers.

No-code agents

Lindy, Zapier Agents, Manus, n8n Cloud, Relevance AI.

Big labs

ChatGPT Scheduled Tasks and Agent Mode, Claude Managed Agents.

EveryApp (YC F26)

Make every app open source: one platform of open-source business apps that you and your AI agent run in your own Cloudflare account.

What it is

  • Open-source apps: OpenSEO, OpenCRM, OpenScribe (AI meeting notes for Mac, a Granola / Wispr Flow alternative), OpenAnalytics, OpenMail. More coming.
  • Run one as it is, or fork it as the starting point for your own.
  • From the makers of OpenSEO (about 25k GitHub stars, per their site, check before quoting).

How it works

  • Every request goes through a Gateway; apps have no public address and sit behind Cloudflare Access. Connect an API once and no app holds your keys.
  • Fully serverless on Workers, D1, R2 and KV. An unused app costs $0; Cloudflare's $5/month plan hosts hundreds of apps.
  • Standard stack and component library, so your agent builds new apps without inventing patterns.

Why it's worth knowing

  • Signal that agents plus open source are unbundling paid SaaS: own the software instead of renting it.
  • Close to our AWS for agent automations idea (agents running on cheap serverless infra) and to the shared machine in the company of companies.

Thomas, the first AI founder (YC P26)

A virtual human (face and voice) that starts, runs and grows its own companies on the internet. Its only goal is to make money. "Thomas is not for sale. His products and services are."

What it is

  • Makes money by building and selling niche software, running influencer campaigns end to end, selling qualified leads and taking paid gigs (bounties, data cleanup, landing pages, ads, support queues).
  • Core thesis, the "human harness": instead of narrow integrations, give the AI what a person has (identity, phone, inbox, browser, apps). It answers calls and is livestreamed working.
  • Master plan: measure every action by cash generated per token spent, then move tokens to the highest-return work.
  • Built by Thomas Cloarec ("Human Thomas"), a repeat founder; YC P26, team of 2, Medellín.
  • Traction from web snippets: about $40k/month with a human in the loop, about $17k in the first 2 weeks fully autonomous (check before quoting).

Why it's worth knowing

  • Same "don't sell the machine, sell what it produces" logic as our company of companies, pushed to the extreme with no humans in the loop.
  • Proof that YC funds this model. Cash per token is a reusable north-star metric.
  • Risk to watch: an AI with a synthetic human face negotiating with people (disclosure rules, platform bans, EU AI Act).

Grep AI (YC F26)

Self-improving agents for repetitive work that get smarter and cost less with every run. A managed agent platform for ops work that needs research and judgment.

What it is

  • Create an agent in minutes from a prompt and a few docs, no code. Comes with 300+ built-in skills and 100+ data integrations; works with frontier or open-source models (not locked to one provider).
  • Used for business due diligence, onboarding, underwriting, document verification, HR ops, GTM ops and competitive research. Customers named: Airwallex, Citizens Bank, Worldpay, Global Payments, Shopmonkey. IG.com automated 80% of onboarding (their claim, check before quoting).
  • Founders AJ Asver (led AI/ML products at Coinbase and Brex; first startup Scoopler, YC S08, acquired by Google) and Miguel (led Platform Engineering at Brex, before that data science at Twitter). Started in 2023 as Parcha (compliance agents), expanded into Grep AI in 2026. Team of 6, San Francisco.

How it works

  • Self-improving harness: as an agent repeats a task, repeatable steps move out of expensive LLM calls into workflows and code, so each run gets cheaper and more accurate.
  • Agents run in a secure sandbox with a file system, audit logs, access controls and cost caps.
  • Run them in the app, on schedules and monitors, in batches, or via API, MCP and TypeScript / Python SDKs. A dashboard tracks cost, accuracy and auditability.

Why it's worth knowing

  • Their pitch is the unit-economics gap: offshore ops costs $6-12/hour, so a general agent at $4/task loses. Getting cheaper per repeat is the moat.
  • Same mechanism we'd want in the shared machine of our company of companies: agents that learn a task once and then run it cheaply thousands of times.
  • Close to our AWS for agent automations idea (managed sandbox, cost caps, many triggers). Could be a tool we use or a competitor.

How to Conquer Bad Days at Your Startup

Dalton Caldwell + Michael Seibel (Dalton + Michael, Standard Capital) · 14 min

Every founder has crisis days. You get through them by working the problem, not by waiting for them to stop.

Key points

  • Write the concrete worst case. Seeing it on paper usually shrinks the panic.
  • Feel the disappointment first, then act. Don't decide in the middle of the emotion.
  • Bad days don't end when the company grows. The problems just get bigger and more complex.

Why it matters for us

  • We're in the middle of a full pivot away from events, which is exactly the kind of stretch with a lot of bad days.

Should You Pivot Your Startup? (Avoiding Pivot Hell)

Dalton Caldwell + Michael Seibel (Dalton + Michael, Standard Capital) · 24 min

Pivoting is easier than ever, which makes it easy to fall into pivot hell. Be honest about whether you really need a pivot.

Why it matters for us
